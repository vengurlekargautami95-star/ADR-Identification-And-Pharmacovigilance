# ADR-Identification-And-Pharmacovigilance


•	Introduction

Medicines help us prevent and treat diseases. However, sometimes they can cause unwanted or harmful effects. These effects are known as adverse drug reactions (ADRs).
Pharmacovigilance helps identify, monitor, and prevent harmful effects of medicines. It plays an important role in protecting patient safety.

•	History of Pharmacovigilance: Thalidomide Tragedy

Thalidomide was a medicine used during the late 1950s to treat certain conditions, including morning sickness in pregnant women in some countries.

Its use during pregnancy was linked to serious birth defects, such as shortened or missing limbs in babies.
This tragedy highlighted the importance of monitoring medicines for safety and led to improvements in drug safety monitoring worldwide.

•	Important Definitions

1.	Therapeutic Effect
The intended beneficial effect of a medicine.
Example: Paracetamol reduces fever.

2.	Undesirable Effect
An unwanted or unfavourable effect associated with a medicine.
Example: Unwanted drowsiness after taking an antihistamine.

3.	Adverse Drug Reaction (ADR)
A harmful and unintended reaction to a medicine for which a causal relationship is reasonably suspected.
Example: Developing a suspected allergic rash after taking an antibiotic.

4.	Side Effect
An unintended effect related to the pharmacological action of a medicine.
Example: Drowsiness caused by certain antihistamines.

5.	Toxic Effect
A harmful effect caused by excessive drug exposure or other factors affecting the body’s response to a medicine.
Example: Liver damage caused by excessive paracetamol intake.

•	Classification of Toxic Effects

A.	Pharmacological Toxicity
Occurs when the normal action of a medicine produces an excessive or harmful effect.
Example: Excessive insulin action causing low blood sugar.

B.	Pathological Toxicity
Occurs when a medicine causes damage to body tissues or organs.
Example: Drug-induced liver injury.

C.	Genotoxic Effects
These effects involve damage to DNA or other genetic material.
Example: Certain anticancer medicines can damage DNA.

D.	Allergic Reactions
These occur when the immune system reacts to a medicine.
Example: Skin rash, itching, or hives after taking a medicine.

E.	Idiosyncratic Reactions
These are unusual and often unpredictable reactions that occur in certain individuals.
Example: Severe haemolysis in a susceptible patient exposed to a particular medicine.

F.	Teratogenic Effects
These are harmful effects on the developing baby when exposure during pregnancy affects development.
Example: Thalidomide exposure during pregnancy was linked to limb abnormalities.

•	Classification of ADRs Based on Time of Onset

Acute- Develops rapidly after exposure
Immediate allergic reaction

Subacute- Develops over days or weeks
Rash after several days of treatment

Latent-  Appears after a longer period
Certain delayed toxic effects

•	Classification of ADRs Based on Severity

Minor- A mild reaction that usually does not require major treatment.
Example: Mild nausea.

Moderate- A reaction that may require medical treatment or a change in therapy.
Example: Persistent vomiting requiring medical attention.

Severe- A reaction that causes major discomfort, serious clinical problems, or significant impairment.
Example: Severe drug-induced liver injury.

Lethal or Fatal -A suspected adverse reaction that results in death.
Example: A suspected medicine-related fatal reaction.

 Severity describes how intense a reaction is. Seriousness is assessed using specific regulatory criteria, such as death, life-threatening events, or hospitalization.

•	Pharmacovigilance

Pharmacovigilance is the science and activities involved in detecting, assessing, understanding, and preventing adverse effects and other medicine-related problems.

	Adverse Event (AE)
An adverse event is any unwanted medical occurrence after a patient receives a medicine, whether or not the medicine caused it.
Example: A patient develops a headache while taking a medicine.

	Serious Adverse Event (SAE)
An SAE is an event that meets at least one recognized seriousness criterion.
Seriousness Criteria
An event is considered serious if it results in:
1. Death.
2. A life-threatening condition.
3. Hospitalization or prolonged hospitalization.
4. Significant or persistent disability.
5. A congenital anomaly or birth defect.
6. Another medically important event requiring intervention to prevent a serious outcome.

•	Causality Assessment

Causality assessment is the process of checking whether a medicine may have caused an adverse reaction.
The assessment considers the timing of the reaction, other possible causes, medical history, and what happened after the medicine was stopped.

Dechallenge -The reaction improves after the suspected medicine is stopped or reduced.

Rechallenge - The reaction returns after the medicine is given again. Rechallenge should not be performed deliberately if it could harm the patient.

	Bradford Hill Criteria
The Bradford Hill criteria are considerations used to help evaluate whether an observed association may be causal.

Bradford Hill Criteria are 9 guidelines used to assess whether a drug or exposure may have caused an adverse effect.

1.	Strength: How strong is the relationship?
2.	Consistency: Is the relationship seen repeatedly?
3.	Specificity: Is the cause linked to a particular effect?
4.	Temporality: Did the cause occur before the effect?
5.	Biological Gradient: Does a higher dose increase the effect?
6.	Plausibility: Is there a scientific explanation?
7.	Coherence: Does it agree with existing scientific knowledge?
8.	Experiment: Does changing or stopping exposure support the relationship?
9.	Analogy: Have similar drugs caused similar effects?

In PV: These criteria help assess a possible causal relationship between a drug and an adverse drug reaction (ADR)..

	WHO–UMC Causality Assessment Scale

It is a method used in Pharmacovigilance (PV) to assess the relationship between a drug and an adverse drug reaction (ADR).

Categories
1.	Certain: Clear relationship between the drug and reaction.
2.	Probable/Likely: Reaction timing is reasonable, and other causes are unlikely.
3.	Possible: Drug may have caused the reaction, but other causes are also possible.
4.	Unlikely: The timing makes a drug relationship improbable.
5.	Conditional/Unclassified: More information is needed.
6.	Unassessable/Unclassifiable: Information is insufficient or contradictory and cannot be verified.

Example: If a patient develops a rash after starting a medicine, and the rash improves after stopping it, the relationship may be considered possible or probable depending on the other available evidence.

	Naranjo Algorithm

The Naranjo Algorithm is a questionnaire used in Pharmacovigilance (PV) to determine whether an adverse drug reaction (ADR) is caused by a drug.

It contains 10 questions, each with a score.
Scoring and interpretation:
1.	9 or more: Definite
2.	5–8: Probable
3.	1–4: Possible
4.	0 or less: Doubtful

Example: A patient develops a rash after taking a drug. The Naranjo Algorithm helps assess whether the drug caused the rash.

Advantages

- Easy to follow step by step.
- Provides a structured scoring method.
- Helps organize information during causality assessment.

Limitations

- Missing information can affect the score.
- Some questions may not apply to every case.
- The score does not prove causality.

•	Pharmacovigilance Programme of India (PvPI)

PvPI is a national programme established to monitor the safety of medicines in India. Its National Coordinating Centre is the Indian Pharmacopoeia Commission (IPC), Ghaziabad.

	Goal of PvPI

To improve patient safety by monitoring adverse reactions and promoting the safe use of medicines.

	Objectives of PvPI

- To collect suspected ADR reports.
- To identify possible medicine safety signals.
- To evaluate medicine-related risks.
- To improve awareness of ADR reporting.
- To support safer use of medicines.
- To provide information for medicine safety decisions.

	Who Can Report?

- Doctors.
- Pharmacists.
- Nurses.
- Other healthcare professionals.
- Patients and consumers.
A person can report a suspected ADR without proving that the medicine caused it.

	How to Report an ADR

1. Identify the suspected reaction.
2. Collect the available patient and medicine information.
3. Complete the appropriate ADR reporting form.
4. Submit it through an authorized PvPI reporting channel or ADR Monitoring Centre.
5. Provide additional information if requested.
Official website: https://ipc.gov.in/PvPI/adr.html

•	ADR Reporting Form

The ADR reporting form is used to record details of a suspected adverse drug reaction.

Important Sections

1.	Patient Information
- Patient initials or permitted identifier.
- Age and sex.
- Relevant medical history.

2.	Suspected Medicine Information
- Medicine name.
- Dose and route.
- Reason for taking the medicine.
- Start and stop dates.

3.	Reaction Information
- Description of the reaction.
- Date of onset.
- Treatment given.
- Outcome of the reaction.

4.	Concomitant Medicines
- Other medicines taken by the patient.
- Relevant doses and dates, if known.

5.	Seriousness Information
-Whether the event caused death, was life-threatening, required hospitalization, caused disability, resulted in a birth defect, or met another important criterion.

6.	Reporter Information
- Name or permitted identifier.
- Profession.
- Contact details, as requested.

•	Important Points While Reporting ADRs

- Report suspected reactions; proof of causality is not required before reporting.
- Provide accurate patient and medicine information.
- Record the date of medicine initiation and reaction onset when available.
- Mention relevant medical history and other medicines.
- Record the outcome of the reaction.
- Do not guess or invent missing information.
- Protect patient confidentiality.
- Report through the appropriate channel.

•	Four Minimum Criteria for a Valid ICSR

A valid Individual Case Safety Report (ICSR) generally requires:
1. An identifiable patient.
2. An identifiable reporter.
3. A suspected medicinal product.
4. An adverse event or reaction.

•	Practical Case Study

Case: A 35-year-old woman develops a rash and itching three days after starting an antibiotic.
Item| Details
Patient| 35-year-old woman
Suspected medicine| Antibiotic; exact name not provided
Reaction| Rash and itching
Time to onset| Three days
Action taken| Medicine discontinued after clinical assessment
Outcome| Symptoms improved
Rechallenge| Not performed

	Assessment

The reaction occurred after the medicine was started, and symptoms improved after it was stopped. These findings may support a relationship, but they do not prove causality.
Further information is needed about the exact medicine, other medicines, medical history, and clinical findings.

	Learning point: Report suspected reactions accurately and use the available evidence to assess causality.

•	Conclusion

Pharmacovigilance plays an important role in monitoring medicine safety and protecting patients. Understanding ADR definitions, classifications, seriousness criteria, and causality assessment helps healthcare professionals identify and report suspected reactions correctly.
The Pharmacovigilance Programme of India supports ADR monitoring in India. Accurate reporting and proper documentation contribute to better understanding of medicine-related risks and safer patient care.
